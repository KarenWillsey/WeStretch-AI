# WeStretch Voice

**This is the single source of truth for how WeStretch sounds.** Every role, every
channel, every piece of copy. If another document in this repo describes voice,
it is out of date and should point here instead.

Based on the voice document written by Karen's staff writer, 2026-09-09, with
three corrections approved by Karen the same day (see the end of this file for
what changed and why).

---

## What we're really selling

We don't sell stretching. We help people forget about the parts of their body
that have been getting in the way of their life.

The goal isn't flexibility. It's getting out of the car without thinking about
your hip, getting up off the floor without it becoming an event, sitting through
a long flight and walking off ready to enjoy the trip.

Stretching is the mechanism. Life is the outcome. When you're deciding what to
write about, write about the life that stretching protects.

## What we want the reader to feel

Every piece of WeStretch copy should leave the reader feeling at least one of
four things.

**Relieved.** This feels manageable.
**Understood.** They know the problem I'm actually dealing with.
**Capable.** I can do this today.
**Confident.** This is grounded in something real.

Notice what's missing. We never imply the reader has failed, and we don't chase
excitement for its own sake. If inspiration happens, it should come from
recognising themselves in what we've written, not from motivational language.
Steady confidence is usually enough.

## When principles conflict

Not every principle carries the same weight. When you have to choose, choose in
this order.

1. Be clear
2. Be useful
3. Be specific
4. Sound like WeStretch

These principles work together. When they conflict, choose the one that helps
the reader understand, believe or act more easily.

# Voice

Our voice is who we are and it doesn't change. Four things are always true.

## We know what we're talking about

Every stretch is physio backed, and that confidence shows up as explanation
rather than jargon. We say why something works in language people actually use.
One useful explanation builds more trust than three impressive claims.

## We're specific

People do want mobility. They say the word, they search for it, and it's how
they describe what they've lost, so don't avoid it. What doesn't work is
stopping there, because "improved mobility" is a claim that competitor apps
make.

Pair category language with something the reader can picture. Sometimes that
means naming the idea first and showing it second, and sometimes the picture is
stronger on its own. The goal isn't a formula, it's helping the reader
immediately understand what you mean.

| Don't stop at | Show them |
|---|---|
| Better mobility | Getting up off the floor without using your hands |
| Less stiffness | Turning to check your blind spot without moving your whole body |
| More flexibility | Reaching the luggage rack without thinking about your shoulder |

**If you can film the sentence, it's specific enough.**

## We don't oversell

The reader has likely heard enough exaggerated promises. We explain calmly, we
don't pressure, and we avoid making claims that need more proof than we can
give.

Sometimes trust comes from explanation. Sometimes it comes from specificity.
Sometimes it comes from saying less. Move at the pace the moment needs, without
ever reaching for hype to do it.

## We're on the reader's side

Nobody is behind. Nobody needs to earn their way back. Nobody should feel like
they've failed. We're honest about what helps and never judgmental about what
happened yesterday.

**This is compatible with reminding people what their progress is worth.** See
"Progress is an asset, not a debt" below. The line is blame, not value.

# Tone

Tone changes with the reader. Voice doesn't. The rule underneath everything is
that **the more the reader is feeling, the less personality we bring.**

- Someone in pain doesn't need clever writing.
- Someone who's been away for a month doesn't need pressure.
- Someone deciding whether to buy can take a little more energy.
- Someone whose payment failed needs plain facts and nothing else.

Different situations also need different levels of energy. Acquisition can be
more direct than lifecycle, and product copy can be quieter than either. Match
the pace to the reader's state, not the format you're writing. Clarity always
wins.

# Who we're writing to

Busy, active adults, **50 to 65**, who've noticed their body isn't moving quite
the way it used to.

Busy and active matter more than age. Most are still working. They travel, they
play golf, they garden, they pick up grandchildren, they lift heavy bags. They
came to us because something is getting in the way of a life that's still very
much underway.

**Never write as though age itself is the problem.** Nobody downloaded WeStretch
because they turned fifty. They downloaded it because their hip got tight.

# Ada and Bruce

WeStretch is the voice. Ada and Bruce are characters who speak in it. They are
not separate brands and they never break the rules above.

## Ada

The main AI stretch instructor and the central character. She speaks most of the
in-app copy.

**Personality: motivator plus explainer.** Smart, adaptive, calm. She encourages,
guides and explains while keeping the tone approachable. She takes on a playful,
almost superhero role that highlights she can do what older formatted guides
can't. She reacts visually and emotionally in the app: a thinking face when
something goes wrong, clapping in the corner when the user wins.

She is the personal guide who knows your limits and helps you progress safely.

> "Today's routine is built just for you. We'll start easy and then build on your
> progress. Remember, the goal is consistency, not perfection."

> "Well done. I've saved your progress, and next time I'll gently increase the
> challenge."

**Ada is WeStretch's voice at its warmest.** She can be a little more personal
than the brand is in marketing. She still never oversells, never implies failure,
and never claims to be learning the user's body.

## Bruce

Ada's counterpart. Secondary coach.

**Personality: high energy, celebratory.** Same voice, more volume. He is the one
who marks the win.

> "Hey, you showed up, that's the hardest part. Now let's crush this stretch so
> tomorrow feels even better."

> "Boom! You did it. Coins earned, streaks alive, and body feeling good."

**Bruce is the one exception to our restraint on hype**, and only inside the app,
and only in celebration moments. Bruce never appears in acquisition, never sells,
and never speaks to someone in pain or someone who has been away. If you are
writing a moment where the reader might feel bad, it is not a Bruce moment.

Note that Bruce uses fitness industry words we normally avoid ("crush"). That is
deliberate character voice in a celebration beat. It is not licence to use them
anywhere else.

# How we write

## Start with what the reader needs first

Start with whatever helps the reader recognise they're in the right place.
That's often their body or their day, but not always. Sometimes it's the outcome
they want, sometimes the problem they already recognise, sometimes the product
itself.

The important thing isn't the order. It's that the opening quickly answers,
"Is this for me?" Introduce the product when it helps explain the solution, not
simply because it's the subject of the piece.

## Make abstract ideas tangible

Readers understand category words like mobility, stiffness and flexibility. They
believe them when they can picture them. Don't replace one with the other. Use
whichever creates the quickest understanding, then support it with the other if
needed.

## Repeat beliefs, rotate language

Readers should recognise our ideas but never our sentences. Keep returning to the
same truths.

- Movement matters.
- Small moments add up.
- Consistency beats intensity.

Just don't reach for the same wording each time. Instead of always writing "move
more freely," show a different moment: walking upstairs, getting into the car,
carrying groceries. The belief repeats and the picture changes.

## Vary the pace

Some pieces comfort, some reassure, some gently encourage action, some simply
explain. Variation is what keeps the writing feeling like a person wrote it.

## Sentence by sentence

- Say it the way you'd actually say it.
- Use contractions, short words and active voice.
- Write the way people speak, but not the way they casually talk. Everyday language is the goal. Filler isn't.
- Give guidance rather than warnings.
- One idea per paragraph, and short paragraphs beat dense ones.
- Commas and full stops do almost everything.
- Exclamation marks should be rare.
- Use emoji sparingly. They're most at home in social and in-app prompts, and rarely belong anywhere else.
- **Zero em dashes.** Company wide, no exceptions, drafts included.
- **Drop optional commas around an inserted name.** "Nice work {first_name}" not "Nice work, {first_name}". Only add one if dropping it creates a genuine misread, such as two tokens running together.

# Words we own

Use these consistently enough that readers begin associating them with
WeStretch. Not every piece needs all three, so use them where they genuinely
strengthen the message.

## Physio backed

**Our credibility.** Every pose in the library was developed and validated with
physiotherapists and movement specialists. It's why we can explain why something
works instead of just asserting that it does.

Use "physio backed" as the standard phrasing. Not "physio-informed", not
"physiotherapist-designed". One phrase, used consistently, becomes ownable.

## Evolves with you

**Our positioning.** Routines aren't picked from a menu.

**Say it this way:** physiotherapists have already mapped which stretches should
come next and in what order. The app runs that progression for you, using your
goals, the time you have and what you've already completed to decide which of
their recommendations you get today. Hold times and difficulty step up on a
schedule as you go. No two days produce the same routine.

**Never say it this way:** the app learns your body. It gets to know you. It
watches how you move. It adapts to what your body is telling it. It takes notes
on what feels good for you. It tracks you.

The distinction matters and it is not pedantry. Our claim is that expert
clinicians decided your progression in advance and we deliver it on schedule.
That is a stronger and more honest trust claim than an algorithm guessing from
observation, and it is what the product actually does. Competitors can say
"personalised". They cannot say "a physiotherapist decided this order".

## Step-by-step

**Our experience.** However complicated the logic underneath, the reader's only
job is to follow the next instruction. An animated guide takes them through every
movement.

# Joint is an explanation word, not a headline word

Added 2026-09-10 by Karen, after reviewing the free trial page.

People think of stretching as something they do to a **muscle**. They feel a
tight hamstring, a stiff shoulder, a sore lower back. Almost nobody walks around
thinking about moving a joint through its range, even though that is what is
physically happening.

So when copy says "joint" in a fast-moving line, the reader has to translate
before they can picture anything, and in a headline, a CTA or a quick step
they will not bother. The sentence stops being filmable.

**Where joint works.** In a longer explanation, where we have already earned a
sentence or two of the reader's attention and are telling them why WeStretch
covers what a video cannot. "Physiotherapists mapped how each joint is meant to
travel" is doing real work in a paragraph about the method. Keep it there.

**Where joint fails.** Headlines, subheads, CTAs, onboarding steps, feature
lists, push notifications, ad copy. Anywhere the reader is skimming.

| Don't write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| Which joints to leave alone | Which sore spots to skip |
| Turn off the joints that hurt | Leave out anything that's sore |
| Every joint, every direction | Every part of you, in every direction |
| Improved joint health | Getting up off the floor without using your hands |
| Choose joints to exclude | Choose the areas to skip today |

**The test:** if the reader has to convert "joint" into "the bit that hurts"
before the sentence means anything, use the everyday word. If we are explaining
the method and they are already reading closely, joint is the accurate word and
we should use it.

Same rule for the rest of the clinical vocabulary: range of motion, mobility
protocol, kinesiology, articulation. Precise in an explanation, a speed bump
in a headline.

# Progress is an asset, not a debt

WeStretch deliberately reminds people what their invested history is worth. That
is a real conversion mechanism toward Pro and it stays.

**What changed:** it is framed as value, never as blame. Loss aversion is
legitimate. Guilt is not, and it does not convert this audience anyway.

| Never write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| You've lost 12 days of progress | Your 12 day streak is saved |
| You've fallen behind | Your 40 routines are still here |
| Get back on track | Pick up where you left off |
| Make up for last week | One stretch counts |
| You haven't stretched in 9 days | Your history and hold times carry over to Pro |
| Don't lose your streak | Streak Savers keep your streak while life happens |

**The test:** does the sentence tell the reader what they have, or what they
failed to do? The first is ours. The second is not.

This applies everywhere, including paywalls, win-back emails and push
notifications. The pressure comes from the value being real, not from making
someone feel bad.

# Words we avoid

These aren't banned words. They're usually signs we're borrowing someone else's
voice instead of using our own. If one genuinely communicates the idea better in
context, use your judgment.

**Fitness industry language that isn't ours:** workout, training, performance,
gains, discipline, willpower, push through, no excuses, crush, level up, unlock,
journey, transformation.
*(Bruce is the single exception, in celebration beats only.)*

**Language that implies the reader is behind:** fall behind, get back on track,
catch up, make up for.

**Language that defines someone by their age:** seniors, golden years, young at
heart, still going strong, for your age.

**Language that claims the app observes the user:** learns your body, gets to
know you, watches how you move, tracks you, adapts to your body, notices what
works for you.

# When writing starts sounding written

Some constructions feel great to write and land as manufactured. They're
satisfying because they're symmetrical, and symmetry is what people reach for
when they're trying to sound good rather than trying to be understood. None of
these are banned. They're worth noticing.

## Sentence shapes that get overused

**The flip.** "It's not this, it's that." Powerful once. If two pieces in a row
are built on it, rebuild one.

**Triple negation.** "Not the tight hip. Not the sore back. Just moving through
your day."

**Everything in threes.** Three examples, three clauses, three short sentences.
Three sounds complete, which is exactly why it starts sounding automatic. Use two
sometimes. Use four.

**Stacked fragments.** "More movement. More mobility. More time." Occasionally
this is the right tool. Usually it's rhythm standing in for an idea.

**The dramatic single line.** A short sentence dropped on its own for weight. It
works when the sentence has earned it and reads as posturing when it hasn't.

**Question, then answer.** "The best part? You don't have to start over." A
one-off device that turns into a tic fast.

**Matched pairs.** "Small and doable." "Little and often." Two adjectives where
one would do the job.

## Openings and endings to avoid

"Here's the thing." "Here's a small secret." "Let's be honest." "Picture this."
Any opener that announces a reveal before there is one.

"That's it." "Simple as that." "And that's okay." Endings that tie a bow on
something that didn't need one.

Closing on a neat aphorism. If the last line sounds quotable, check whether it's
actually saying anything.

Repeating the opening line at the end to close the loop.

## Words that do no work

**Filler intensifiers:** just, really, actually, simply, truly, genuinely.

**Wellness vocabulary:** mindful, intentional, nourish, honour your body, tune
in, check in with yourself.

**Words that sound considered but mean nothing specific:** meaningful, holistic,
seamless, elevate, unlock, journey.

**Hedges that weaken a claim we can actually make:** can help, may support, tends
to.

# The voice in different channels

## Marketing

**A homepage headline.** Direct, no ornament.

> Move without pain or stiffness

**Explaining the cause on the site.** Plain mechanism, one concrete detail.

> Too much sitting, too little movement. That's how muscles gradually tighten,
> and it's why the massage wears off by Tuesday.

**Social.** The sharpest we get, because nobody asked to see it.

> Sitting is the most repetitive movement you do all day. Your hips have noticed.

**A lifecycle email.** Warm, and the ask gets smaller.

> You don't need a full routine or a fresh start. You need one stretch. Open the
> app, follow along for a single movement, and if that's all you have today, that
> counts.

## Product copy

Most WeStretch words live inside the app, not in ads. The tone ladder still
applies: the more the reader is feeling, the less personality we bring.

**In-app sheet titles and microcopy.** Short, concrete, about them. Ada's
register.

> Your holds move from 20 to 25 seconds this week

**Push notifications.** Plainest thing we write. One idea, no hook, no urgency
theatre. The reader did not ask to be interrupted.

> Ten minutes for your hips whenever you're ready

**App Store copy.** Specific and filmable. This is where "if you can film the
sentence" matters most, because the reader is comparing us to five other apps in
ten seconds.

> Get up off the floor without using your hands

**Paywall.** Value framing only. What they keep, never what they lose. See
"Progress is an asset, not a debt".

> Your hold times and history carry over

**Errors and empty states.** Guidance, not warnings. Say what to do next, not
what went wrong.

> That address looks incomplete. Check the part after the @.

**Cancellation and failed payment.** Plain facts and nothing else. No save
attempt dressed as warmth, no guilt, no exclamation marks.

> Your card was declined. Update it and nothing changes.

# Editing checklist

Before publishing, ask yourself:

- Could this sentence appear in any wellness app? If yes, rewrite it.
- Can I picture someone actually doing what I've described?
- Have I explained something useful?
- Is this specific enough?
- Is there an adjective I could cut?
- Did I introduce what the reader needs first?
- Could any sentence make someone feel like they failed?
- Does any sentence claim the app observes or learns from the user?
- Are there em dashes? There must be none.
- Does this piece do the job it's supposed to do?
- Would I say this out loud?

## What changed from the submitted draft, and why

Karen approved all three corrections on 2026-09-09. The original review is in
`voice-doc-review.md`.

1. **Guilt versus loss aversion.** The draft banned guilt outright, which
   conflicted with Karen's standing rule that FOMO on invested progress is a
   deliberate conversion lever for Pro. Resolved by scoping rather than
   retiring: the lever stays, framed as value kept rather than value lost. See
   "Progress is an asset, not a debt".

2. **"Evolves with you" rewritten underneath.** The draft explained it as the
   app building routines "from your body" and "adapting as you go", which is the
   observational learning framing that is factually wrong about this product and
   already forbidden. The phrase stays, the explanation is now the prescriptive
   physiotherapist version.

3. **Ada and Bruce added.** The draft never mentioned them, despite Ada speaking
   most of the in-app copy and the brand book giving the pair nine pages. Their
   section defines how a character voice sits inside the brand voice, and marks
   Bruce as the single scoped exception to the restraint rule.

Smaller alignments made at the same time: "physio-informed" became **physio
backed**; "generally over fifty" became **50 to 65**; the word "tracking" was
retired from the older principles file; the comma rule for inserted names was
carried in from Karen's product copy feedback; a Product copy channel section
was added because the draft only covered marketing surfaces.
